# Guide: Building a House (USA)

RULES TO FOLLOW — Building a House in USA
- Secure the land title and verify zoning regulations before you begin design work.
- Hire a licensed general contractor for the build phase — they coordinate permits, subcontractors, and inspections.
- Get permits from the local building department before starting any construction — unpermitted work creates problems later.
- Budget at least 20% contingency on top of estimated construction costs for unexpected issues.
- Hire an architect for design and a structural engineer for foundation and load-bearing elements.
- Plan for 6–18 months from start to completion depending on size, complexity, and permitting.

RULES TO AVOID / RED FLAGS — Building a House in USA
- Don't start construction without clear title to the land — unresolved ownership disputes can halt a project entirely.
- Avoid hiring contractors without a written contract and proof of license and insurance.
- Be cautious of vague contracts that don't specify materials, timelines, or payment milestones.
- Don't assume all costs are covered — utilities connection, landscaping, and site preparation are often separate.

MATERIALS — Typical for USA Residential Construction
- Framing: wood/timber is standard for most single-family homes.
- Foundation: poured concrete or concrete block, depending on region and soil conditions.
- Roofing: asphalt shingles are most common; material choice affects insulation needs.
- Insulation: type and required R-value depends on climate zone — colder regions need higher-rated insulation.
- Exterior: vinyl siding, brick veneer, or fiber cement siding are common options.

WHERE TO SOURCE — USA
- Large hardware/building supply chains (Home Depot, Lowe's) for general materials.
- Local lumber yards and building material suppliers often have better pricing and selection for bulk/framing materials.
- A general contractor typically sources specialty materials through their own supplier relationships.

USEFUL LINKS — USA
- Property records in the US are county-level, not national — there is no single federal registry. Check with your specific county assessor or recorder's office for property records and title searches.
